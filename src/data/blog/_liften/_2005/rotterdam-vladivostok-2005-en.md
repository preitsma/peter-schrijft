## Day 1: Rotterdam - Polish border

In the morning, I stood up at 6.30 and said farewell to my girlfriend Petra.  
Before 8:00, I got my first semi-official ride of the unequalled Jan Bass from the Weena petrol station The Finch. There I intended to hitch with a placard 'Vladivostok', you never know, but it turned out I had forgotten my drawing book to sketch hiking boards. My first error.

Then I asked a ride to some people who were refueling and after 10 minutes I managed a lift to filling station Bijleveld at De Meern plane for Utrecht. The driver was lively chattering who was living in Tholen. He worked 5 days per week at home because his boss gave him no travel expenses to compensate for traveling expenses.

I asked at Bijleveld another hike and got one to a filling station near Apeldoorn. The driver proved to be an Oud-Beijerlander (the town in which I was born) who was working for Getronics. As an It-er, myself I also have been working for this company some time ago. He was account manager for the sector care.  
The petrol station where I was set down was situated at the E30 (A1). This road runs from Amsterdam to Moscow and I must not leave this one for the time being.

Got at that station by asking a direct ride from an Italian who speeded in his minibus in the direction of Germany. He spoke some English and I understood that he stayed the weekends in the Netherlands, and during weekdays at his wife and children in Germany. He was a road worker. He dropped me at a Raststätte before Hanover.

For lack of a hitch board, I approached drivers, which improves the chances. Moreover, you choose this way yourself your driver instead of being chosen. This is all the way more safely too. The next lift I got from Daniel, an inhabitant of Arnhem (a Dutch town) who for his work tidies up houses and hotels in Germany. He had much respect for my venture.  
At Braunschweig he would leave the road. Just before he put me down at the filling station Zweidorfer Holz, a passing customs minibus halted us. There a routine research followed where we both had to leave the car. The customs submitted at first my backpack to a very thorough search. They found much interesting matter but nothing forbidden. At Daniel, however, they hit home. He had hided a big joint in its tobacco bundle. Furthermore, they found there a small pocket with four pills. Daniel told the officers these were medicines for his eye pains. He had put them in a small plastic bag, because he therefore did not need to take along with him the complete pillbox...

![Zoll](_img/1/zoll.jpg)  
The customer's car proved to be a mobile laboratory and the pills were subjected to a range of chemical tests. Something colored red which indicated amphetamine. Daniel got nothing of it but agreed rapidly with the 50-euro fine and the seizure of the found stuff. The agents recommended him to leave the medicines in the usual packaging and not to keep it in drugs like small pocket in his shag bag.  
I aided spiritually Daniel a little. He was considerably fed up of course by so much injustice. Together we still head a coffee and then bade each other farewell.

After short time got by means of questioning a lift to Berlin from two Ossi's who drove back home from the Hurricane festival (Rammstein, Queens or the Stone Age). They were real rockers with a lively car full of empty beer bottles and deafening rock music from the speakers. I gave them my card with my internet address so they could follow my travels.

I had myself put off on Raststätte Michendorf where I in former days, in DDR-times, sometimes had been with my parents to spend the last Ostmarks on glassy potatoes and hairy soup. This time I have nothing eaten there.

The next ride was with two Russians in a minivan. They reacted full disbelieve when I told them did my travel plans. The lived already 13 years in Germany and according to me they had someway lost the feelings with their homeland.  
They brought me to a petrol station and adjoining lorry terminal about 3 km behind the border in Poland only not in my intended direction but more aiming at Zeleny Gorod.  
There I made during about two hours for all freight carriages on the giant terminal, but nobody headed my direction. They went or to Southern Poland, or to Germany or continued to spend the night there. This did not make any progress. After two hours waiting, I managed a strategic lift to the border with a lively Polish lorry driver without semi-trailer. Its lorry rattled on all sides and had large problems with finding correct acceleration. The driver cursed lively (Kurwa) but got its truck moving with much bumping in the direction of the border.  
![Tent eerste nacht](_img/1/tent_eerste_nacht.jpg)  
Plane for the border stood two Polish young police officers of about 18 years of age, which prohibited me to hitchhike so near to the border. That was rather shitty because now had to hitchhike along the road, a couple of hundred meters from the border, on a spot where everyone exactly was very well accelerating. It shimmered already and I had still no hitching board. I decided to pitch down the tent in this still little left daylight. This succeeded.

The end of the first day, everything went according to plan and I traveled about 900 km from the start.

## Dag 2: Polish border – 50km before Belarussian border

The next morning I awoke early and rolled up the tent. When I stood at the road, again, the same young police officers stood there also and I still was not allowed to hike nearer to the border. The spot was miserable and therefore I decided after 10 minutes to walk in the direction of the terminal where I already had been rooted the previous evening.

![In Polen](_img/2/inpolen.jpg)  
Then suddenly a Lithuanian lorry stopped which went into my intended direction and I was on my way!  
Driving did not go fast because of lack of a highway, much traffic, and dawdling by Alexandr. He had to drink frequently coffee and was searching for special offices which according to him were situated somewhere along the way. There special bills for expenses were fabricated which he possible could claim at his boss. After having even driven back for a stretch he at last found a likeably office somewhere behind a demolition firm at the side of the road.  
It already was halfway the afternoon when I had Alexandr figured that it would make him a difference of 50 km. if he would drive by Warszawa in stead of taking a more northern route. He agreed and we kept on driving a stretch. On about 50km distance of Warszawa it appeared however that this road was prohibited for freight lorries. I did not know this of course. I was put down at a filling station to find myself at the wrong side of the road and left the lustily in Polish, Russian and probably also Lithuanian cursing Alexandr behind me.

![Alexandr](_img/2/alexandr.jpg)  
There were on these spot three options:

1. Hitchhiking beside the highway at an unfavorable spot
2. Hitchhiking at the petrol station at the wrong side of the road
3. Hitchhiking at the trucker's restaurant.

A violent thunderstorm broke and therefore I chose for option 3. The driver sat the restaurant did not drive into my direction and one of them advised me even to return to The Netherlands.

I stayed until the end of the shower and took a bite and a drink. When it was dry again, but already as much as a little twilight, I still tried to get a lift on the unfavorable spot. After 30 minutes there, a hyphen code scanner salesperson stopped in an ordinary car who spoke English well. He told he already had left standing two hitchhikers that day. Once he had been collided from behind, he had pasted down his bumper with duck tape. However, it seemed to him it would improve his luck to take me along. He took me to the outskirts of Warszawa and on the way; we considered that it would be most skilful for me to take bus 125, which crosses the complete city from West to East. This way said, this way done. It was not possible to buy a ticket in the bus, so it was a free ride.

On the East side of Warszawa, I took another bus (502) which still went a couple of kilometers easterly to a filling station. That went rather smooth. The spot was favorable, but it was only meanwhile 22:30.  
After half an hour, asking a Polish boy in a minivan proved to be very delighted to take me along. He was with a friend on the way from Frankfurt Main to Eastern Poland. The driver was very tired and needed much coffee.

![Twee Polen](_img/2/tweepolen.jpg)  
He told me he had driven 6000 km in 7 days in his minivan. That is something. Anyway, we had much fun and by means of shortwave, they made jokes with other nocturnal drivers. At 1:30, I was set down about 50 km from Terespol (the border of Belarus). There with authorization of the owner of the petrol station I pitched my tent on a beautiful spot. Satisfied I fell asleep.

## Day 3: 50km before Belarussian border - Minsk

![Nacht 2 in tent](_img/3/nacht2intent.jpg)
In the morning, I awoke at 7:30 and rapidly scored short lifts directions Terespol and behind. There I found myself standing hitch hiking on the wrong track because I misinterpreted a traffic sign. Then I found out I had to go simply straight ahead. I rapidly got a lift from a Polish couple to Brest near Belarus.

![Verwarrend bord](_img/3/verwarrendbord.jpg)
Taken off with them I had also to have to go through the complete border procedure, which lasted 3 hours. There also stood still a convoy of 30 Belgian and Dutch campers and caravans, which were on holiday in the direction of St. Petersburg and Moscow. On the other side of the border I got a short lift from two fat, shorthaired Russian boys to a good hitchhiker spot on the other hand of Brest.  
On the way I examined Brest from the car. The hitchhiking spot had been well populated with grouchy Russians, who probably were not hitching hiking for fun. I qued for good manners. After about 30 minutes, standing in the hot sun an old man in a decrepit Ford picked me up, whereas the other hitchhikers were still not all gone. The old man drove in the direction of Minsk. He had an appointment halfway with someone who came from Minsk. This one would be able to take me further according to my driver and this occurred. The two had a short shadowy discussion after which I could mount a brand new Toyota bus of the other man form Minsk. He was a young paunchy character who had much interest for the Netherlands. He brought me to the hotel in Minsk where I arrived before 18.00.

![Minsk outskirt](_img/3/minskoutskirt.jpg)

## Minsk

As some people told me, Minsk was indeed a very clean town. Hardly any litter and dust on the streets. The town had seen a total destruction in WW2. This gave the Soviets the opportunity to raise a model communist town: broad alleys, a stadium down in the centre of town, fast squares, parks and public gardens where all the seats were occupied, and no church buildings and furthermore rather dull from a touristy viewpoint.  
The museums are not very interesting, except the war museum.  
Eye-catching also is the lack of much advertising in the streets, much less than in Moscow for example. Most bus shelters are furnished with posters about the commemoration of the victory over Nazi-Germany. That theme lives on.

Minsk has been provides with a metro system with two lines and one can see tiny trams and trolleybuses. No much traffic is seen in the streets. However, as I already said so, everything is clean and well kept. No sings of dilapidation or dirty grubbiness as in other post-soviet towns.

I found a well working cash point and bought a SIM-kartochky. I spent my first night in a hotel. The booking could be advanced. I am one day ahead of my conservative travel scheme.  
During my first shower in three days, I found a tick on my breast, probably caught in the Polish wood. The Xenos tick tongs got his baptism of fire and worked well. Now I have to wait for signs of Encephalitis. However, the risk is rather small.

After I explored the city June 17, in the evening it was my time to look for the address I booked via Hospitality Club. I already phoned my host, but noticed little enthusiasm. After I found Alexey, he turned out to be very cordial. It appeared that my voice through the phone sounds as Arabic-Russian and he was afraid he had to deal with an Arab.

Alexey is a computer science student and he will graduate next week. He lives in the heart of the town together with two other computer science students. He has a job at IBA, a company doing business in offshore programming for westerns customers. Their biggest customer is IBM. IBA formerly was part of IBM. They made a content management system for an English customer. He demonstrated it and it looks very nice: it was possible to define object types and the relations in between them.  
He is also occupied by an old Russian self-defense sport and he is a drummer. He had a job for one year in Miami to earn money for paying his study. His housemates also were working to pay for their study. One of them even worked in the building trade in Naples, Italy.

Striking was neither of them had a bed. They are sleeping on a blanket on the ground. Because of this, they have by day more living space; however, the rooms were not small at all. In the whole house, no television set is to be found. All three of them do have a modern PC with the works.

## Day 4: Minsk - Moscow

In the morning, I stood up at 7.30 and after a fast cold shower said farewell to Alexey. Then with the underground railway to station Bostok and there I took bus 99 to the terminus. When we drove to the last bus stop I saw already a hitchhiker standing, flat behind a police station. I decided to walk there and just have a chat with the hitchhiker.  
Exactly when I address him, a car stopped for him stopped. The hitchhiker asked the driver if he would take me along too and that was possible. The driver, Dima, appeared to have lived a year in Antwerp and had been a couple of times in Rotterdam of which he had clear and nice memories.

It appeared that he directly drove to Moscow! So, that was settled. The other hitchhiker was called Vjaza and was 19 years old. He remembered my mail on the Russian Backpacker forum and was very glad to meet me! He invited me immediately to come to Jaroslavl. He repeated the invitation several times during our travel and enforced them by adding with all kinds of descriptions of this 1000 years old city and its curiosities. It sounded tempting although it, however, was situated 250 km in the wrong distance.

![Dima, Anya en lifter](_img/4/dimaanyaenlifter.jpg)  
In Belarus Dima drove rather steady because he had no Belarusian money for petrol. We refueled with my last money and then we could drive faster.  
I made myself already up for a heavy border crossing but this proved to be out of the question. However, there was a fast lorry traffic-jam and private cars could keep on driving without passport control. Belarus and Russia forms a type of union or something like that. There are unfortunately as much as two visas necessary but the forms, which I had filled at the Polish - Belarusian border, is valid for both countries.  
Once we reached Russia, the speed increased rather well and from time to time, we reached a180 km-speed on the excellent motorway. The other hitchhiker had told me that maximum speed until then had been 160; therefore, it was an extra reason for Dima to raise this high watermark. Furthermore not unsafe.

After a very sociable ride of about 8 hours, we reached Moscow. Everyone was glad. The other hitchhiker was put down at an underground railway station and Dima started asking me what plans my plans were. These were rather vaguely, I must admit, I had a list with phone numbers and hotel charges, but concrete things I had not yet considered. Then he invited me to his house.  
That proved to be a complete nicely wide apartment in Southern Moscow. Rapidly even took a shower and after I had ironed my trousers on insistence of Dima went we to a Ukrainian restaurant.

![Anya en Matilda](_img/4/anyaenmatilda.jpg)

A little bit of vodka, Borsch, and Salo (blank animally fat that in the Ukraine is considered as a delicacy). On the way to home, I still bought some DVD's and breakfast for the next day. At night drank some whiskey and examined the portfolio of Anja examined, who does model work. Driving through Moscow, she designated her colleagues on the billboards. Furthermore, we watched the film Madagascar: a must. I got for a present a DVD of the film Fandorin, from the novel of Boris Akunin (Fandorin, a corruption of the Dutch name Van Dooren, is the Sherlock Holmes of Russia) and a large bottle strong Belgian beer, which proved to be later German and of which Oleg got rid of at breakfast.

Fallen satisfied asleep in a super bed.

We had the usual evening and day with Oleg, very enjoyable if it does not last too long. Oleg had not been in the centre of Moscow for 20 years. In the evening, he returned to Kiev.

It is high tide I leave this again and exchange my hotel room for a place on the floor, to replace my fresh armpits with full-bodied sweat and to find quickly some hitchhikers to give me useful information for my route.


## Meeting Russian hitchhikers

Yesterday I found a new place to stay with Lena, who lives in a spacious apartment right on the outskirts of Moscow. After a pleasant meal at her place, we went out together last night to look up a few Russian hitchhikers, which she also found interesting.

First we met Sergei Karitich, webmaster of [hike.ru](http://www.hike.ru), the Russian hitchhikers site. He spoke excellent English, since he works as an interpreter-translator. He had never hitchhiked to the Far East of Russia, but had done so many times to Western Europe, always travelling via Finland from St. Petersburg.  
I agreed with Sergei that I'd report my progress to him every now and then, which he would then post on Hike.ru.

![Lifters](_img/moscow/lifters.jpg)
With him we then went to look up a certain Oleg. Oleg is the tallest Russian I've met so far (>2m) but otherwise looked like a garden gnome. He was still working at an archive right near Red Square. Oleg hitchhiked from Moscow to Cape Town last year — hats off to that.  
He's a 'well-known' member of the [AVP](http://www.avp.travel.ru/), the academy of free travel, in Moscow. Membership is by invitation only, and only comes up once you've clocked more than 100,000 km of hitchhiking experience.  
He gave me a number of very useful little books. The information in them is also on the AVP site, but these booklets are handier to have on the road. All the booklets were written by [Anton Krotov](https://www.boekreporter.nl/index.php?itemid=63) (1976), who has by now hitchhiked to many far corners of Russia, and has also hitchhiked through Africa, Afghanistan, Iran, Syria, and China.

1) **The practice of free travel**. Krotov's first book, and a really good introduction for any Russian who feels like travelling without spending a fortune on it. It covers subjects like choosing the best route, techniques for stopping cars in all kinds of circumstances, formulas for working out your daily speed, how to find places to sleep in unfamiliar cities, how to get food cheaply, how to arrange a free shower, what the best gear is, how to behave when staying with strangers, etc.  
   The AVP's slogan is 'наука победит', which roughly translates to 'science conquers'. The single most important and only absolutely essential piece of gear, the book argues, is your own 1500 grams of brain.  
   I already knew this book, but it's great to have it now in a fixed, handy format. Krotov is a good author who knows how to write engagingly. Thanks to his booklets, I've already missed my stop on the metro twice today.

2) **134 questions and answers**. Krotov self-published his first book and sold it in huge numbers at festivals, concerts, and on trains. It really struck a chord with a certain group of young people and with poorer (ordinary) Russians in general (a relatively large group). The success was overwhelming, and Krotov became a role model for many. His 'followers' are also known as 'Krotovniki', and honestly, I'm one of them too.  
   Krotov publishes his home address, email, and phone number in all his booklets, and he soon had to take all sorts of measures just to get a bit of peace and quiet for himself. One of those was writing the second booklet, in which he answers 134 questions he's frequently asked. A selection of the questions:

- Do you often end up in unpleasant situations?
- Do accidents involving hitchhikers happen often?
- Do you ever lie?
- Do hitchhikers ever die?
- What do you do when drivers ask for money?
- I want to travel with you!
- Is it hot in Africa?
- Are there countries poorer than Russia?
- Do you ever drink vodka?
- How do I hitchhike in winter?

etc. etc. Really fun to read, and nice to have too.

3) **The encyclopedia of 'free' travel**.  
   This book is the most useful one for me. It consists of 277 pages in A5 format, completely crammed with barely readable tiny print. It describes all the major roads, cities, hitchhiking spots at every city and how to reach them. It has tips for cheap places to eat, sights worth seeing, and it tells you, for every city, where the permanent hangout spots are where you can meet kindred spirits. It contains at least as much information as the Lonely Planet for Russia, which I left at home this time. A truly excellent book.

4) **All the elektrichkas of Russia**  
   From every city of any significance in Russia, electric trains run connecting the city with its immediate hinterland. They often go 100 km or further out from the cities. These so-called elektrichkas form a large network, and in the European part of Russia it's often possible to travel from city to city via the elektrichka network, if you have patience. Elektrichkas are only loosely conducted, so it's often possible to ride for free too.  
   This book is also in A5 format but uses an even smaller typeface. Just barely legible, it lists all the departure times of every elektrichka, and believe me, there are a lot of them.

I paid a small amount for them; later, once I saw everything I'd actually been given, I thought a bit more would have been fitting, but there'll still be a chance to pay more at the festival. Armed with these books I feel a lot more confident.

The AVP has set itself the goal of providing information about free travel to anyone who's interested. So I talked through the rest of my trip with Oleg, and we made an inventory of the difficulties and possible dangers awaiting me:

1) **Alcoholics**. Found all over Russia, but tend to wander along the roads in Siberia rather often. Drunk drivers are quite rare, according to Oleg (alcoholics have usually already sold their car, and the police are very strict about drink-driving). Advice: stay calm, don't shout at them, don't talk to them, and calmly walk away.

2) **Ticks**. Advice: cover your skin when walking through a forest, and check yourself thoroughly when you come out again. If after a while you start feeling weak, you might have caught the disease. In that case you need to see a doctor.

3) **The Chita–Khabarovsk stretch**. There's no asphalt there, and hardly any traffic at all — a few cars per hour, or even fewer. Advice: have patience.

4) **Police**. Officially, the law still exists that you must register in any city where you don't live. This also applies to Russians themselves. In practice, this is of course not feasible for a traveller/hitchhiker.  
   In European Russia it's all more or less tolerated, but in remote areas this law can sometimes be followed rather irritatingly strictly by the police. Advice: have patience, or, if unlucky, get sent home by plane at your own expense.

## Day 5: Moscow - Penza

The journey from Moscow to Penza (600 km) went well. In the morning, I awoke early and took a shower. First, I had to remove the cat litter from the bath. After an hour traveling by metro and bus, I stood at the starting point of the M5. This road runs from Moscow, by way of Penza and Samara to the Urals to Cheljabinsk. Overall, it is a stretch of 1800 km.

![Christen](_img/5/christen.jpg)
After ten minute Anatoli halted, who took me along to the village of Lychovitsji, some 120 km. Anatoli appeared to be a fanatic Christian. His car was hanged with little icons. In addition, when I told him about my present journey he crossed himself and repeated this dozens of times during our ride.

At each church we passed he crossed himself, but also sometimes at his own liking. He was on is way to a 'starets'. This is the name of a mystic seer/saint with special gifts and a broadband connection with the spiritual world. You could call him an orthodox version of a medicine man. On our way, Anatoli wanted to visit the grave of an other starets, called Nila, who died shortly and now would quickly being canonized. As we approached the church, Anatoli crossed himself elaborately. A priest who walked there was kissed on the hand. In the church Anatoli kissed the icons.

Behind the church, a special little building had been erected wit the grave of Nila in it. It was covered with sand and circled by vases full of flowers.

Anatoli suggested I would ask her to bless my journey, but I did not like this. He went into the building, sat on his knees for a long time, and pressed his head on the sand, which covered the grave. He put a beautiful rose in one of the vases and again kneeled. After this, he went to the special shop and bought a photograph of Nila, an old woman with hanging cheeks. The photograph he pushed some times again his face and pinned it down in his car. Instead of giving money to the beggars at the church, the al received a banana from him. I must honestly say I became a little bit ill from this much devotion and piety.

When we drove further on, he told me about Christianity in Russia. Since 2000 years, nothing had changed. That is why it was the only true Christianity. He told about the dyxovol mir, de spiritual world around us, which is filled with little green devils. Devils of prostitution, of alcoholism, etc.

Anatoli told about Russian women seeking western men by way of contact agencies. He compared this with a pan full of fresh milk. After some time the best nice light foam is floating, under that rests the milk and the dirty lumps of fat are sinking to the bottom. Women who are looking for a western man are those dirty lumps.

On our way, we passed the exit to the village of Gzelj. During my former hitching trip, people told me that the blue Delft wooden shoes, which I gave as presents, are not typically Dutch at all. They are Russian souvenirs named Gzelj. Anatoli told me that Peter the Great after his journey to Holland, had ordered to build a factory here to produce china from Dutch examples.

I got off in the hamlet Lychovitsi and Anatoli blessed me. I was very decent of him you give me the phone number of some of his acquaintances in Chita. Maybe it would come in handy later on.

In Lychovitsi hang a funny banner: Russia has three capitals: St. Petersburg, Moscow and Lychovitsi.

Quickly I got a ride again in a little truck with a sober driver but not so sober passenger, who liked some company. Alexander the driver was an Okay man with a refined opinion about foreigners, Muslims, integration policy, etc. Instead of taking the ring road, we drove especially through the town of Rjazan, so I could take some pictures. He offered me some times vodka (medicine as he called it) but I refused. He did understand this and thought this sensible. A stretch behind Rjazan I was dropped off.

The next ride followed quickly. It was a rather silent soldier on leave in a Zjigula car. We drove thorough magnificent scenery until Zubova Poljana, situated in the autonomous republic of Mordovia with the capital Saransk. There I had to wait an hour for the next ride. Some stray dogs roamed there of which I am not a fan. I walked some hundreds of meter further on where road works were carried out at a bridge over the river Vod. All cars had to halt at a traffic light over there and not before long, I could get in a truck which usual transported cars. When I got in it appeared a drunken man was in the sleeping berth. He was the brother of the driver who came along with him. He drank a whole bottle of vodka in Moscow.

I noticed again that Russian who did not drink themselves, because they had to drive, had quite fun in company who had drunk alcoholics. The driver's cabin was quite a mess, but both men were nice company and asked a lot about my journey and about Holland. Lied again about my age and income.

The driver was a look-alike of Kevin Kostner; His drunken brother was a butcher and told me over and over what a superb brother he had and how much he loved him.

The lorry had no freight and on some stretches drove 120 km/h. I asked if no limiting device was on the vehicle. Kostner told me he had let installed a special button with which the device could be switched off. Plain on the dashboard.

Nature still was marvelous and we passed tiny villages with churches of which the shape varied from dilapidated to recently restore with shinny cupolas, which could be seen from a far of distance, glistening in the sunlight.

Near Penza I got of the truck and the M5. With a taxi to the town centre and found a decent hotel. The end of a superb day.

## Day 6: Penza - Tjoletti

After a day in Penza, I left in the evening town by elektrichka to the M5. De elektrichka ran very slow and it was not before 21.00 I arrived in the village of Chaadevka. It was still a walk of 500 meters to the hitching spot, but I decided to pitch my tent instead of hitching. A whole army of midgets pestered me, but they did not come into the tent. I received SMS's from Lena and Jan de Bas. Some noisy creatures crept along the tent, but luckily no homo sapiens.

I awoke at 8.00. The weather was rather overcast with tempting rain. Near my lift spot, on top of a hill where drivers could see me well, a police car stood. That was no invitation for cars to halt and I walked a stretch further on. The drizzle became a downpour and I sheltered under a lean-to. When the rain stopped the police car drove away and I quickly returned to my hitching spot.

## Grushinski Festival

This year the Grushinski Festival is held for the 32nd time. In 1965, the boy Valeri Grushinski drowned in the Volga when he tried to save the lives of children who had fallen in to the river. The children were rescued, but Valeri himself drowned. Grushinski was a student in air- and space technology who played the guitar very well and could marvelously sing.

To honor him his friends organized every year a meeting with singing and music. This developed into a major festival with sometimes more than 300.000 visitors. In the eighties, the communists forbade the festival.

People visiting the festival are drinking very large amounts of alcoholics.

After two days at the festival I have to say, not withstanding my reserves, it has become a real social adventure. We had a sociable chat and he knew rather much about the area. This was much Taiga with low jaunty hills which are locally called 'sopki'. In western Russia, hills are all called 'Cholmi'. Along the way, we passed a launching site for nuclear missiles, which we could not see from the road.

Three kilometers before Bikin the police station was situated, which I could examine very well, because I had to wait for three hours on this very spot. Many midges, heavy showers and the few cars, which passed (70 cars an hour), were all entirely packed. The last miles are the longest. When I took shelter under a lean-to at the police station, I was able to examine the work of the officers at close range. Some 90% of the time they played cards and after each game, ending in loud cheering and cursing, they put on their caps, went outside and stopped some cars for checking documents.

The police officers wore dirty uniforms and behind every police station, one could see a well-kept vegetable garden with potato plants. Overall, during my trip I did not get the impression that being a police officer in Russia is an enviable job.  
I decided to ask them whether they could assist me. The police officer noticed I was a foreigner and asked for my passport, more for interest than for a sense of duty. After he had examined the Dutch travel documents with much interest, he went inside again for a new play of cards and said: 'We'll find something for you.' I heard him inside calling to his colleagues: 'Gollandets!'  
Some minutes later from the distance a big truck appeared. The policeman came outside and halted it. After a short talk, I could get in and so I got a ride to Ussurisk, a town 100 km. before Vladivostok. I asked myself whether this was really hitchhiking, was it the policeman who halted the truck or was he only the instrument in my hands and was it a real lift? I did not give it more thought.

The driver of the American made truck was one of the most skilled and sturdy chauffeurs I ever met. The trailer was empty and he tore at 90 km/h, and sometimes at 100 km/h, over the bumpy road. It seemed like he knew every hole and bend. He told me he regularly drove to Yakutsk, the capital of the regional republic Sacha. Neither a railway nor an asphalted road runs there. He told about the expeditions with a number of trucks, in which they carried Chinese audio-video-devices to Vladivostok in winter with temperatures below 40 over the gruntovka's. 3500 kilometers in 15 days.

He did not share my dislike for the Kamazzen, this were quite good vehicles. He did not like European trucks, because these seemed to break down at temperatures below 40 and anyhow, they soon broke down after riding some 100 kilometers on roads without any asphalt. The driver was a hard character. He earned enough, had been to Japan to buy a Japanese micro van and went with his wife to Thailand on holiday. The last stretch we drove through the dark, which was no reason for him to diminish his speed. On the way, we got some water and bought apples.

At 2.30, I was dropped at the police station before Ussurisk, still some 100 km. to go. I had become very tired and decided to have a nap in my tent for the last time. It was not easy to find a good spot. All good fields and meadows, which I saw from the cars on my way, were at closer look ankle-deep swamps. At a filling station was a decent little lawn and there I pitched my tent with permission of the gas station keeper, also thanks to my LED head lantern. Brushed my teethes, demidgeted my tent and went to sleep.

In the morning at 7.30 Zhona, the service station attendant, woke me up. He thought I could not stay there because of the danger of the underground tank, which was at a distance of 10 meter from my tent. He was very friendly and utterly surprised to find a Dutchman in the morning at his workplace. He assisted me in wrapping up my tent, which had excellently withstood the showers that night. When everything was wrapped up, it started raining again and with hindsight, I was lucky Zhona woke me up.

I hanged around still a while at the gas station, where some friends of Zhona, who were rather under the influence, came sauntering along to have a look at their working friend. I asked whether they were students, to which they offensive answered that they were already 25 years old and now they already were unemployed.  
I got my raincoat and umbrella and went heading to the DPS. Because of the rain and the many puddles it was difficult to hitchhike and quite soon I decided to bring the policemen -my best friends- into action again. The officers stood under their shelter and, seriously gladly surprised by my request, set at work, after someone of them asked me carefully to show my passport.

At once, they halted a big Nissan station wagon with a Chinese or Korean Russian speaking family in it. The driver did not show any grumpiness because he was pushed to take along a sleepy, wet and not all together armpits freshly Dutch hitchhiker. He halted at several spots of which he thought they would be interesting photo opportunities. It was pouring and in Ussurisk we saw rather some vehicles, which slid from the road. On our way, the 3-year old daughter had to vomit because of carsickness.

In the low ceilinged car I hardly could keep open my eyes; I was obviously very tired. From my experience the 120 kilometers which we had to go to Vladivostok, we covered very fast. At the gate of Vladivostok, I again got the opportunity to take pictures. A pole is standing there with a quote from Lenin: 'Vladivostok is far-off, but it really is our town!'

We drove into town and I was dropped at a bus stop. There I immediately asked someone to take a picture of me, because I felt a very big natural smile appear on my face.

## Vladivostok

In Vladivostok, I had a large choice of addresses to stay the night. I had a lot of phone numbers and e-mail addresses. In Khabarovsk I already mailed and sent a sms to one Lena, who invited me a month earlier by e-mail. She read my query at bpclub.org. I decided to agree with her invitation and this appeared to be a fortunate choice. After she picked me up at the station in her right hand driven Nissan, we drove to her house, which was a little out of town along the M-60. She lived there with her husband and son in a, to Russian and Dutch standards, big and luxurious detached house with garden and banya.

Lena and her husband Andrej were the best boarding family I could wish. They did not force anything upon me and did feel exactly what I needed. They were very refined people, intellectuals and both were journalists. Lena worked at a university publisher and Andrej at a local TV-station. Six months ago, Anton Krotov had stayed with them and I slept in 'his' room.

I wandered two long days through Vladivostok. The town had been founded in 1860 and is notwithstanding the large distance to Moscow and the nearby China, Japan and both Korea's, an unmistakable Russian town, spreading over some hills at the Pacific coast. Some buildings in the city originate from pre-revolutionary period. In spite of the usual lack of maintenance, the excessive advertising signs and the continuously traffic jams, here and there you can see some beauty. The Jugendstil railway station for instance is in good condition and has a fairy-tale radiation.

I spent my time visiting museums, viewing a submarine stuffed with Chinese tourists, walking, surfing the net, sheltering from the rain, shopping and buying an airline ticket for Amsterdam. The last task was not easy at all; my credit card (MasterCard) was accepted nowhere. Only at Tuesday afternoon I succeeded to cash 10.000 rubles and Wednesday morning another 20.000 for paying the ticket. After that, I could not get any cash anymore and had to break into my money, which I had brought with me.

Wednesday afternoon together with Lena I visited the editorial office of the local paper Novosti, which, tipped by Lena, would like to interview me. One Julia asked the questions and I answered. There were no questions I had not been asked before and I was able to answer rather fluently in my sloppy Russian. Only the question what the first thing was I would tell at arriving at home, I could not answer. After showing my website, on which in the mean time enough information is seen for filling a number of Saturday's supplements, they were very satisfied. The result is printed already and can be read [online](http://www.novosti.vl.ru/?f=sz&t=050729sz01) and I actually recognize something of my answers.

That same evening was the festively and well reported pouring out of the water form the North Sea into the Pacific Ocean. Andrej and Lena drove me to a surprisingly beautiful beach, some 20 kilometers from Vladivostok, situated at a bay. The emptying out went very well and we observed a quickly mixing. No sifting or fluffy foaming. The North Sea and Pacific Ocean molecules put up with each other excellently. The Pacific lived up to his reputation. After that at the coast, I had a swim and juggled once more.
